## 🎙 PODCASTS
Takeaway: Arca, a $64M AI-native wealth management firm, runs on a model-agnostic harness where agents proactively prompt human advisors rather than the reverse.

On Latent Space, Aman Singh, who leads the AI platform team at Arca, explained that the firm is not a SaaS company but the firm itself, using AI to replace the work around the advisor rather than the advisor. Clients do not interface with AI directly; they experience service delivered through human advisors while agents handle back-office analysis around the clock. The firm uses frontier models from Anthropic and OpenAI, with Claude as the primary model, but decisions are guided by internal benchmarks. Singh said external benchmarks claiming great things for Luna did not pan out: "It was way too slow or... And it was costing a lot." Arca keeps a model-agnostic harness and assigns heavier model tiers to complex tasks like tax analysis while lower tiers handle background activity.

Long-horizon work spans about ten days. In a tax bill example, agents analyze where a client's money is allocated and the optimal way to liquidate $100,000, but strict guardrails keep humans in the loop for money movement, liquidation, and direct client communication. Safety is deterministic at the edges: every database write gets an LLM-based verification, prompt injection is detected at input edges, and outbound data passes PII checks plus a consensus of small models to prevent exfiltration. Auditability is by default, with every agent and human action producing a change log.

Singh's memorable line: "Agents prompt people more than people prompt agents." Memory systems track what is happening for each client, what is being tracked next, and who owns which action, with a nightly "dreaming" reflection updating state so a fresh agent catching a new signal, like a phone call, can ramp up on an ongoing tax bill. Arca is bullish on Anthropic and OpenAI models but wary of managed agents, since owning memory and intelligence means owning control of the business. Feedback from staff edits is piped into a data store paired with agent traces, and a nightly meta agent suggests harness changes, though a human engineer still reviews prompt changes because agents "will make really nasty prompt changes if you leave them to it."

**Takeaway**: Legal AI is less about raw model intelligence and more about orchestration, evals, and codifying expertise into durable systems.

Jacob Lauritzen, CTO of Legora, joined Latent Space to discuss building legal agents when you can't see the data. He traced Legora's origin from a simple ChatGPT with Swedish laws and a search tool into a full agent that drafts, edits Word files, organizes documents, and runs complex analyses. The product now includes **Tabular Review** for bulk extraction across up to a million cells, **Lists** (described as Linear for legal work), **Intake** for client collaboration, and **Portal** for lawyer-to-lawyer and agent-to-agent collaboration.

On models, Lauritzen said Legora uses a mix depending on the task and subdivides problems to route accordingly. There is a model selector for the primary model, though he is unsure it will stay. He estimated about 60% of the work can be done really well right now, with the rest needing more intelligence, especially for complex analysis.

The most striking moment came on evals: "We had one problem where it wouldn't cite properly, and so we were like, 'Next model release solved.' It went from like 70% to like 99%." He said the evals team, a mix of three engineers and three lawyers plus a pool of legal engineers (Big Law lawyers with CS degrees), grew out of the assistant agent team. Model releases often improve some practice areas and regress in others, which the heat map tracks.

On the profession, Lauritzen pushed back on the idea that paralegals and junior lawyers simply disappear. He argued good junior lawyers are worth more than ever, the billing model is shifting from hourly to project fees especially for lower-end work, and the partnership model may need to change so partners are incentivized to codify expertise into durable firm IP. ## 🎙 PODCASTS
The takeaway: the virtual cell is graduating from mapping and prediction to actively steering the next biological experiment.

On the Latent Space AI for Science podcast, RJ Hanocki and Brandon Anderson speak with **Fabian Theis** of Helmholtz Munich about controlling biology. Theis traces the arc of his field: early human cell atlases handled mapping, then world models tackled prediction, and now the goal is steering experiments. He introduces **Lab Compass**, an inverse generative model that, given a forward world model, tells you what you need to do to reach a certain state. The ultimate vision, he says, is to RL everything, including in the loop, though he admits it is a bit out there.

Theis reflects on why virtual cells did not take off earlier: datasets and the questions asked on top were too simplistic. His lab began perturbation prediction modeling with a 2019 paper called **SCGen**, a nonlinear model doing a linear transformation in latent space, and even now many pseudo linear approaches still do a good job. What changed is data scale. He points to **Pars Bio**'s giga lab measuring ten million transcriptomes in a single experiment, and to screens now spanning hundreds of cell lines with full CRISPR knockouts or batteries of a thousand drugs, making the perturbation space suddenly rich.

He explains the fundamentals for non-biologists: each cell carries a roughly twenty thousand dimensional vector of messenger RNAs, the transcriptome, and perturbations are simply inputs to the cell, from CRISPR knockouts to media conditions and signaling. He is convinced the field needs to move to multicellular systems, including organoids like beating heart cell clumps and mini brains that start connecting and computing, to get truly interesting readouts.

On modeling philosophy, Theis is candid about the bitter lesson. Literature shows strong a priori inductive biases can cost predictive power, and AlphaFold is his example: early models built in rotational invariance, later versions found symmetry automatically with more data. In data poor regimes like perturbation space, hybrid models may still win, but he expects more flexible data-based models to dominate, with interpretation layered on afterward. He also stresses that reviewers now demand proper scaling laws, noting his contrastive learning paper **SC concept** shows strong scaling, with a 350 million cell model up to about a hundred million parameters on Hugging Face.

His most memorable line captures the shift: "From mapping, we went to prediction. But now we can go to actually steering the next experiment."
